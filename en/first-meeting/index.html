Your first AA meeting in Medellín

If you're thinking about walking into a meeting, here is what to expect. No sign-up, no cost, and nobody will ask you to explain yourself.

The essentials

  • There is only one requirement: a desire to stop drinking. You don't have to be sure you're an alcoholic, and you don't have to have hit bottom.
  • It's free. No registration, no dues, no attendance lists. Some meetings pass a basket for a voluntary contribution (rent and coffee); you can let it pass.
  • It's anonymous. First names only. Nobody asks for ID, nobody contacts your family or employer.
  • You can just listen. You don't have to speak or introduce yourself. If you like, "I'm new" is plenty.
  • Arrive a few minutes early and, if you can, stay a moment afterwards: that's when people come over to welcome a newcomer and swap numbers.

English-speaking meetings in Medellín

There are 17 English-language meetings a week, in El Poblado, Laureles, Belén and Sabaneta, plus Zoom meetings you can join from anywhere. They're run by English-speaking expats and travelers and welcome visitors; you'll usually find people who have been sober a long time and others on their first week. The map lists each one with its address, time and a contact.

If none fits your schedule, there are also 138 Spanish-speaking groups, most of them meeting every evening around 7:00 pm. Many are glad to have a foreign visitor even if your Spanish is basic (see the phrases below). Switch the map to "All" to see them.

What happens in a meeting

A meeting runs about an hour (Spanish groups often an hour and a half). Someone opens by reading the preamble and the Twelve Steps, then members take turns sharing their experience: what drinking was like, what brought them to AA, what life is like now. No cross-talk, no advice, no judgment. Meetings close with a short prayer (the Serenity Prayer); you don't need to be religious or join in.

Open vs. closed meetings

A closed meeting is for people who have, or think they may have, a drinking problem. An open meeting is for anyone: family, friends, students, health professionals. If you're coming for yourself, either one is fine. If you're accompanying someone, choose an open meeting or ask at the door.

Worried about being seen?

Common, and understandable in a city where the expat community is small. You can start with an online or hybrid meeting (marked on the map; camera off is fine), or pick a group across town. Nobody from AA will acknowledge you on the street unless you do first: anonymity works in both directions.

If someone you love drinks

AA is for the person who wants to stop; nobody can be made to go. For family and friends there is Al-Anon, with meetings in Medellín and online in English (al-anon.org). You can go with someone to an open AA meeting, but looking after yourself usually helps more than anything.

Newcomer questions

Do I have to say I'm an alcoholic? No. Give your first name, or say nothing at all.

Can I come if I've been drinking today? Yes, as long as you don't disrupt the meeting. Plenty of people arrived at their first meeting that way.

Do I have to pick a home group? Not now. Try a few; each has its own feel.

What should I bring? Nothing. Some coins or small bills if you'd like to contribute, and a notebook if you're the type.

What if I'm late? Slip in quietly and sit down. Nobody minds.

Is AA religious? No. It talks about a "Higher Power," which each member defines for themselves; there are atheists and agnostics in the rooms.

I'm only in Medellín for a week. Is it worth going? Yes. Visitors are a normal part of the English-speaking meetings, and a meeting is the fastest way to meet sober people in a new city.

If you're in a medical emergency (seizures, hallucinations, severe shaking after stopping drinking), call 123 or go to an emergency room: severe alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous and needs medical care. AA doesn't replace a doctor, but it's there afterwards.
