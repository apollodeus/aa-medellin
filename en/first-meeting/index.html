New to A.A.?

If you seem to be having trouble with your drinking, or if your drinking has reached the point where it worries you, you may want to know something about Alcoholics Anonymous and what to expect at a meeting.

Only you can decide

No one in A.A. will tell you whether you are an alcoholic. That is for you to determine. If you drink more than you mean to, if you have tried to stop or cut down and could not, or if drinking is causing trouble at home, at work or with your health, A.A. may have something to offer you. The only requirement for A.A. membership is a desire to stop drinking. There is nothing to sign, there are no dues or fees, and no one will ask you why you came.

If you are not sure, A.A.'s pamphlet "Is A.A. for You?" has twelve questions only you can answer.

What A.A. is

Alcoholics Anonymous is a fellowship of people who share their experience, strength and hope with each other that they may solve their common problem and help others to recover from alcoholism. Members meet in groups. A.A. is not allied with any sect, denomination, politics, organization or institution, and is self-supporting through its members' own contributions. Some meetings pass a basket; you are welcome to let it pass.

Meetings in Medellín

There are 17 English-speaking A.A. meetings a week in Medellín, in El Poblado, Laureles, Belén and Sabaneta, and on Zoom. They are run by English-speaking members who live here or pass through, and visitors are a normal part of them. Each one is on the map with its address, time and a contact number.

There are also 138 Spanish-speaking groups registered with AA Colombia across the metropolitan area, most of them meeting every evening at 7:00 p.m. You are welcome at any of them; the meeting will be in Spanish, and a few words are enough to be understood (see below).

What happens at a meeting

An English-speaking meeting here runs about an hour; Spanish-speaking groups usually an hour and a half. The meeting opens with the A.A. Preamble and often a reading from A.A. literature. Members then share, in turn and without interruption, their experience with alcohol: what it was like, what happened, and what it is like now. There is no cross-talk and no advice-giving. Meetings generally close with the Serenity Prayer; no one is required to take part.

You do not have to speak. You may simply listen. If you are asked, your first name and "it's my first meeting" is enough. It is customary for members to come over afterwards to welcome a newcomer and offer a phone number. That is how A.A. works.

Open and closed meetings

Closed meetings are for A.A. members only, or for anyone who may have a drinking problem. Open meetings are for anyone interested in A.A., including family, friends, students and professionals. If you are coming because of your own drinking, you are welcome at both. If you are accompanying someone, choose an open meeting or ask at the door.

Anonymity

Anonymity is the spiritual foundation of A.A. First names only are used at meetings. A.A. keeps no attendance records or case histories, and what is said in the meeting stays in the meeting. Members do not disclose outside A.A. who attends. In a city where the English-speaking community is small this matters to many people; if you would rather not be seen at a particular group, there are others, and there are online and hybrid meetings, marked on the map.

What A.A. does not do

A.A. does not make medical or psychological diagnoses, provide detox, hospitalization, medication or treatment, follow up on or try to control its members, offer religious services, or accept money from outside sources. If you have stopped drinking and have severe shaking, seizures or hallucinations, go to an emergency room or call 123: severe alcohol withdrawal needs medical care.

For family and friends

A.A. is for the alcoholic who wants to stop drinking; no one can be made to go. Family and friends of alcoholics have Al-Anon, a separate fellowship, with meetings in Medellín and online in English: al-anon.org.
